Lab 1 · Structure · about 20 minutes

Protein Structure Explorer

AI tools like AlphaFold can now predict a protein's 3D shape from its sequence. But a prediction isn't a measurement. In this lab you'll put a structure measured in the lab next to the AI's prediction and learn to read the AI's own confidence score.

  • Compare an experimental structure (Protein Data Bank) with an AI-predicted one (AlphaFold).
  • Read pLDDT, AlphaFold's per-residue confidence score.
  • Explain why low confidence often points to biology (flexible or disordered regions), not just AI error.

Your tasks

Start with lysozyme. Compare the two shapes. How much of the prediction is dark blue? Then look at the counts: the AI model has 147 residues but the lab structure has 129. Find the orange tail. (Hint: the first 18 residues are a signal sequence that's cut off before the enzyme does its job.)
Switch to p53. The lab structure covers only part of the protein. Look at the AI's prediction: where is it confident, and where is it orange?
Switch to insulin. The AI predicts the whole precursor protein (110 residues), but the lab structure has two short chains. Which parts are low confidence, and why might that be? (Hint: parts of the precursor are cut away in the mature hormone.)
For each protein, note the mean pLDDT and the share of residues below 50. Which protein would you trust the AI on most for planning an experiment?
Reflect and discuss
  • AlphaFold's confidence is about its own prediction, not about whether the protein binds your drug. What would you still need to test in the lab?
  • Low pLDDT often marks regions that really are floppy in the cell. How would you explain that to a colleague who says "the AI got it wrong"?
  • Where in your own work could a predicted structure save time, and where would it be risky to rely on one?
