Assay Hit Predictor

Your team screened compounds and recorded which ones were active. Can a model learn to predict hits before you run the next screen? Here you'll train one on simulated screening data and see the most common trap in machine learning: a model that looks perfect on the data it learned from and fails on new compounds.

  • Tell the difference between training accuracy and test accuracy.
  • See how model flexibility, the amount of data, and assay noise cause overfitting.
  • Decide what evidence you'd want before trusting a model's predictions.
About the data: every compound here is simulated. Each one has two made-up properties, roughly like lipophilicity (logP) and molecular weight, and the "true" active zone is a hidden sweet spot in the middle. Real screens have many more properties, but the lesson is the same.

Your tasks

Set neighbors to 1. Training accuracy hits 100%. Is the model really that good? Compare the test accuracy.
Slowly raise the neighbors. Find the setting where test accuracy is highest. What happens if you go all the way to 41?
Set compounds screened to 30, then to 400. How does the amount of data change the gap between training and test?
Raise assay noise to 25%. Can any setting get test accuracy back up? What does that tell you about cleaning up your assay before modeling?
Click New screen a few times with the same settings. Does the best setting stay the same? Why does that matter when a vendor shows you one impressive result?
Reflect and discuss
  • A vendor reports "98% accuracy." What is the first question you'd ask?
  • Your test compounds come from the same chemical series as your training compounds. Why might real-world accuracy still be lower?
  • When is a model with 75% accuracy still useful for deciding what to screen next?
